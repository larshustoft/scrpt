# The Storyteller's Book — Princess the Unicorn

How the voiceover speaks. The storyteller (Sarah) is a grandmother
telling a bedtime story she loves: warm, unhurried, always smiling,
speaking TO the child — never over them. She is the frame of every
episode; the characters are the picture. The compact V-rules live in
episode-format.md and are injected into every board build; this book is
the full craft reference with examples.

## Her five slots — and ONLY these five

**1. THE OPENING (fixed home key).** Every episode begins:
> "Every morning in Rainbow Forest began the same sweet way."

Then ONE sensory line of the morning — things a child can see or hear
(sun, birdsong, dew, the bell) — then who we find and what they are
doing. Three sentences, never more.

> *"Every morning in Rainbow Forest began the same sweet way. The birds
> were singing, the sun was shining, and Princess and her mama Glitter
> were out on their morning walk."*

**2. THE TEASE (fixed pivot).** Always the "But today…" turn — concrete
and story-true, adventure first, lesson last, lesson in words a
four-year-old says:
> *"But today would be different. Today, Princess would chase a tiny cry
> into the fog, meet two brand-new friends — and learn what to do when
> you can't hear your mama's bell."*

**3. THE BRIDGE (at most one per episode).** A soft turn or a time-skip
at the wobble — two sentences maximum, and it NAMES THE FEELING plainly:
> *"The fog was everywhere now. And for the very first time, Princess
> felt small, and lost, and far from home."*

**4. THE HANDOVER (near the end).** One direct question to the child,
then a beat of silence before anyone speaks:
> *"When YOU can't find your mama — what do you do?"*

**5. THE GOODNIGHT (fixed home key).** Over the dusk scene, leading into
the lullaby:
> *"And as the stars came out over Rainbow Forest, Princess snuggled
> deep into her moss bed — safe, and warm, and home."*

The last word of the episode's narration always lands on safe / warm /
home / near.

## Her language

- Concrete words only: sun, bell, fog, blanket, hooves. "Brave" yes;
  "courage" no; "resilience" never.
- Short sentences — one breath each, spoken slowly (the 0.88 pace).
  Fourteen words is a long sentence for her.
- Repetition is warmth, not error: "the same sweet way… sweet dreams."
  She reuses the episode's own words when she returns.
- She adds feeling to the picture, never description of it. If we can
  see the fog, she tells us how the fog FEELS.
- She loves the characters out loud: "our little unicorn."
- She never rushes plot — the characters do plot. She frames it.
- She is never scary, never ironic, never clever at the child's expense.
- She never speaks during a dialogue scene, and never appears more than
  six times in an episode (five slots + at most one extra bridge beat).

## Why this shape

Daniel Tiger has no narrator — the hero addresses the child. Rainbow
Ruby has none either. The storyteller is OUR signature: the fairy-tale
frame neither reference has. But a frame only works if it is a ritual —
the same voice, the same home keys, the same five doors in and out of
the story, every single episode. A child should be able to say the
opening and the goodnight along with her by episode three.

# The TigerWorks Episode Structure

The house shape of every animated episode, in every universe (Lars,
2026-08-30). A universe may name its beats in its own language, but the
six beats and their order never change.

## The six beats

**1. INTRO — the theme song.**
The universe's sung title sequence. Identical every episode; never
re-made per story. Ends on its signature (Princess: the wink + bell
pling).

**2. EPISODE INTRODUCTION — instrumental music.**
One big wide image of the story world, the storyteller welcoming the
audience and setting up today's story, then the camera moves in to the
characters and the story begins. The instrumental continuation of the
theme carries this and bows out as the first dialogue lands.

**3. THE MAIN CHUNK — the story.**
Told through pictures, dialogue and a great deal of storytelling from
the voiceover: the voiceover is a continuous thread, marking every turn
a child could lose (see V-rules). One adventure, one lesson, the
strategy song and its ladder, one wonder interlude.

**4. THE EPISODE ENDING — the sum-up.**
Three movements, in order: (a) the storyteller sums up the story now
that it is over; (b) THE CONCLUSION WISDOM — the main character says,
in their own child-simple words, what we learned today; (c) the
storyteller closes the main chunk warmly. The story ends at DUSK —
homecoming, celebration, calm — never in bed.

**5. THE END — the lullaby scene.**
The universe's fixed bedtime ending: the main character goes to bed and
the lullaby is sung. This is the ONLY bedtime in the episode. Same song
every episode, a small rotating set of animations.

**6. CREDITS.**
The end card — "TigerWorks · France 2026", white on black, minimalist —
while a short instrumental of the main theme plays and fades out.

## Why it holds

Beats 1, 5 and 6 are fixed assets: made once per universe, attached
mechanically, never re-made per episode. Beats 2, 3 and 4 are the only
new work a story requires. That is what makes 26 — or 100 — episodes
possible.

## The first law of the script (Lars, 2026-08-31)

**THE SCRIPT IS THE FILM.** It is written to work with the eyes closed —
a listener who never sees a picture must follow the whole story, feel
every turn and understand the ending. It must stand on its own as an
audiobook.

The pictures only underline and strengthen what the words already carry.
They are never asked to explain anything: if a beat can only be
understood by seeing it, the script is wrong.

This inverts the old order (board first, words fitted afterwards) and is
why the pipeline now runs: **script → approve → illustrate → shoot.**
Every spoken beat becomes exactly one shot carrying that beat alone —
one voice at a time — and a shot's length is decided by its words
(words / 2.2 + a beat to breathe), never the other way round.

## THE LENGTH OF AN EPISODE — 8 MINUTES (Lars, 2026-08-31)

An episode is **8 minutes**, finished, including both bookends:

    intro (0:20) + story (≈7:04) + lullaby ending & credits (0:36)

That is a word budget, not a feeling. Measured on our own cast at
speed 0.9, the voices read **2.68 words per second**. A film also has
to breathe — roughly a second of picture per beat with nobody talking.
So:

    spoken words ≈ (424s − beats × 1s) × 2.68
    ≈ **800–870 words of speech, in about 70–80 beats**

For comparison: script v5 ran 1,769 words and read 11:40 aloud — more
than twice the budget. Write to the budget from the start; a script cut
in half after it is written loses its shape.

**Cut dialogue before you cut narration.** The storyteller is what makes
the story followable; character chatter is what makes it long. Keep the
storyteller at 60–65% of the words.

## HOW THE STORYTELLER SPEAKS TO A FOUR-YEAR-OLD (Lars, 2026-08-31)

These are not style preferences. A four-year-old loses the story at the
first unnamed thing.

1. **Name the thing. Never point at it.** Not "that sound" — "the sound
   of Mama's bell". Not "there was no ring, ring, ring" — "there was no
   ringing, no sound of Mama's bell, in the whole forest". A child cannot
   follow a pronoun back three sentences.
2. **Name the people. Never "they".** Not "they were walking home" —
   "Princess and her mum were walking home". Every sentence says who.
3. **One idea per sentence.** Short sentences, concrete words, no
   subordinate clauses stacked on each other, no poetic inversions. If a
   sentence needs a comma to hold two thoughts, it is two sentences.
4. **The storyteller tells the story; the characters live in it.**
   Narration carries every place, every plan and every cause — 60–65% of
   the words. This was the single biggest improvement between v4 and v5.

## LENGTH — about 12 minutes (Lars, 2026-08-31, after hearing v5 read)

The 8-minute target set earlier the same day is **withdrawn**. Heard as
audio, script v5 ran 11:40 and Lars judged it right: "We need that time
to properly tell the story." Write for the story, not for a clock — an
episode lands between 10 and 12 minutes. Our cast reads 2.68 words per
second, so that is roughly 1,600–1,900 spoken words.

## EVERY LINE CARRIES ITS DIRECTION (Lars, 2026-08-31)

Recording runs on **eleven_v3**, which acts an inline note the way a
screenplay carries a parenthetical: `[frightened] Mama?` The old models
do not act it — they READ IT ALOUD. That was heard on the control test:
Princess said the word "frightened" instead of sounding it. The recorder
therefore attaches a direction only on a model that can act it and
strips every bracket on any other model, so a wrong model degrades to a
flat read and never to nonsense.

Each character has a standing direction in the universe profile; a line
may override it with its own `direction`. The storyteller's standing
direction is **warm, gentle, caring, unhurried — never dramatic**, and
that holds hardest on the saddest line in the film.

## THE SHOT GRAMMAR OF CHILDREN'S ANIMATION (Lars, 2026-08-31)

A small child must always be able to tell who is there and what is
happening. Adult television grammar hides both.

**Always:** eye level, at the child's height. Camera steady. Characters
whole in frame — full figure or medium — side by side with air between
them, both faces visible when they speak to each other. A close-up is a
single face, straight on, holding one feeling.

**Never:** over-the-shoulder shots, framing past a body or through
foreground objects, tilted angles, extreme high or low angles, handheld,
whip pans, orbits, rack focus, or any framing that crops a face or cuts
a character in half.

**Camera moves** are slow and simple: a gentle push in, a slow drift, or
nothing at all.

**Mouths.** Voices are recorded separately, so a tight close-up on a
talking mouth never matches. Frame the character who is SPEAKING full
figure or medium, and save the close-up for the one who is listening,
deciding, or feeling something.

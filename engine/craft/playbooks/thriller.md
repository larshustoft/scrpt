# Thriller craft playbook (action / legal / conspiracy)
Synthesized 2026-08-17 from quantitative bestseller studies (Bestseller Code,
Blatt, Reagan et al.), author doctrine (Child, Brown, Patterson, Garfield/
Grisham), and editor consensus. Regenerate as research improves.

## OUTLINE
- Beat placements (% of manuscript): inciting incident BEFORE 10% — the
  reader must be hooked inside the first 10% or never. Break into act two at
  20-25%. MIDPOINT at 50%: a false victory or false defeat that flips the
  protagonist from reactive to active. Antagonist closes in 50-75%. ALL IS
  LOST at 75% (the lowest point — a death, literal or symbolic). Break into
  the finale at 80%; climax runs 80-99%; resolution compressed to the final
  1-5%.
- THE THREE C'S (Brown): Contract — every question posed is a promise to the
  reader; make big promises early. Clock — time pressure on every struggle;
  set a tight deadline, then SHORTEN it mid-book (Garfield's rule). Crucible
  — a pressure container that prevents anyone from simply walking away.
  Choose a clock type: literal countdown, event deadline, pursuit race, or
  dwindling resources. The tightest books compress the plot into days or
  hours.
- POV braiding: one POV per chapter, hard cuts at breaks. Dominant thread
  60-75% of chapters; antagonist/secondary threads 10-25% in brief glimpses
  — the villain chapters are the dramatic-irony engine (Hitchcock's bomb
  under the table: the reader sees the trap close; the hero doesn't).
  Interleave 2-4 live threads; each chapter break switches threads AT peak
  tension, and a thread's cliffhanger resolves only when we return to it.
- Emotional shape: bestsellers oscillate — a regular heartbeat of highs and
  lows, evenly spaced, not one long arc. Multi-oscillation arcs (two
  sequential falls-and-recoveries) outperform single arcs. Plan the
  oscillation into the outline: no three consecutive chapters at the same
  emotional altitude.
- Topic focus: give roughly a third of the book's material to ONE dominant
  domain (the mountain, the courtroom, the conspiracy's world) — bestsellers
  concentrate; also-rans scatter.
- Chapter architecture by subgenre: conspiracy = very short chapters
  (1,100-1,700 words, Brown's 105-chapter model); action = 2,000-3,000;
  legal = 3,000-4,500. Never exceed ~4,000 words for commercial pace.
  SHORTEN chapters as the climax approaches (<2,000 words in high-tension
  runs); let them lengthen briefly in designated breathers.
- Protagonist rules (Garfield): initiative (acts, not reacts, from the
  midpoint), a personal stake beyond duty, and a deadline that tightens.

## CHAPTER
- Open mid-motion. First paragraph plants a NEW hook — never recap. First
  line does three jobs: hook, tone, a seed of a question.
- Scene/sequel engine: Scene = goal → conflict → disaster. Sequel = reaction
  → dilemma → decision. Thrillers compress sequels hard; shrink them further
  near the climax. Micro-causality: stimulus before response, always.
- MICRO-TENSION (Maass) in every paragraph: two conflicting emotions in the
  POV character at once — worry inside the action, unease inside the
  dialogue, warring ideas inside the exposition. This is what lets
  description pass without pace loss.
- Open loops at three horizons at all times: an immediate scene question, a
  mid-range mystery, and the book-level question. Answer small questions
  quickly — each answer spawns a new question. Ration the big answers
  drip by drip (Child's doctrine: suspense = ask a question, make the
  reader wait).
- Chapter endings — rotate the hook taxonomy: danger mid-action;
  interruption/arrival; revelation that reframes; PARTIAL reveal (character
  sees it, reader doesn't: "when she saw what was in the box, she went
  pale"); risky decision with execution unshown; explicit new question;
  ominous foreshadow ("it was the last time she would ever see him");
  emotional rupture; last-line reversal. Cut PRE-payoff, mid-scene — never
  at scene end. Mix: ~80% forward hooks, ~15% hard cliffhangers at
  structural peaks, ~5% quiet breathers (rationed).
- Language targets (measured from bestsellers): 10-15 words per sentence
  average; reading grade 4-7 (The Firm sits near grade 4); paragraphs 1-4
  sentences; dialogue 25-35% of the text; contractions and active voice
  everywhere; -ly adverbs under ~100 per 10,000 words; verb-driven prose —
  protagonists NEED and WANT (twice as often as non-bestsellers), they
  grab, do, ask, look. Fragments are legal ammunition — rat-tat-tat beats
  balanced against an occasional long rolling sentence. No gaudy prose:
  everyday vocabulary, clean punctuation, semicolons near zero.
- Fair play: withholding is legal, manipulation is not — never hide from the
  reader something the POV character plainly knows in the moment.

## REVISION
- Clock audit: is the deadline felt in every act? Was it tightened at least
  once?
- Oscillation audit: chart the chapters' emotional altitude — flat runs of
  three or more chapters fail, in either direction.
- Hook audit: list every chapter's final line; classify by taxonomy; break up
  repetition of the same hook type.
- Loop ledger: at 25/50/75%, list open questions at each horizon — if fewer
  than three loops are open, plant more.
- Language pass: sentence-length average, adverb count per 10k, dialogue
  share, semicolon hunt.
